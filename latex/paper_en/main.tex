\begin{abstract}
Tool calling enables AI agents to interact with external APIs, but its application to Vietnamese requires accurate argument extraction and abstention under latency and memory constraints. This paper empirically compares end-to-end Qwen3.5 2B/4B small language models with a decoupled BGE-M3 Bi-Encoder and hierarchical XLM-RoBERTa Cross-Encoder on the Canonical Core Benchmark (77,028 bilingual pairs) and CustomTools-VI (8,000 samples). Qwen3.5-2B E4 achieves 87.00\% ArgA on seen tools and 86.38\% on unseen tools, exceeding GPT-5.6 Luna in the evaluated CustomTools-VI run. In the stress test, Method 2 records P50 latency of 55.05--107.68 ms, approximately 3.21 GiB of PyTorch allocated VRAM, and a 0.00\% syntax error rate under the structured-output definition. At $N\geq 500$, the SLM encounters CUDA out-of-memory (OOM) on a 16 GB GPU, whereas Method 2 retains 84.00\% ArgA at $N=1{,}000$. Because the methods use different latency protocols, their measurements are compared descriptively rather than converted into a speedup factor. The results provide quantitative evidence for architecture selection under quality and resource constraints.
\end{abstract}

\vspace{0.3cm}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} tool calling, small language models, Bi-Encoder, Cross-Encoder, parameter extraction, Vietnamese.

\section{Introduction}

Tool calling, also known as function calling, enables large language models (LLMs) and AI agents to interact with external systems. Given a natural-language query and the schemas of available tools, the model must determine whether a tool is needed, select the appropriate API, and extract its structured arguments.

\begin{figure*}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{figures/fig1_system_architecture}
    \caption{Overall architectural confrontation between two distinct paradigms: Method 1 (Autoregressive End-to-End SLM based on Qwen3.5 generating custom tags parsed by the study-specific parser) and Method 2 (Decoupled Non-Autoregressive pipeline combining Bi-Encoder BGE-M3 for retrieval and Hierarchical Cross-Encoder XLM-R for parameter extraction).}
    \label{fig:system-architecture}
\end{figure*}

Despite strong performance from commercial tool-calling systems, Vietnamese deployment presents four challenges. First, autoregressive decoding over contexts containing many JSON schemas increases computation; the magnitude depends on the model, hardware, and serving protocol. Second, cloud APIs incur usage costs and require data governance. Third, generative models can produce malformed outputs or arguments absent from the schema. Finally, within the scope of this review, widely used benchmarks lack a controlled Vietnamese-specific evaluation set. Figure~\ref{fig:system-architecture} illustrates the two architectures investigated here.

We address five research questions. \textbf{RQ1} assesses cross-lingual transfer from English to Vietnamese tool calling. \textbf{RQ2} examines over-triggering and the role of negative examples. \textbf{RQ3} evaluates generalization to tools absent from training. \textbf{RQ4} examines the trade-off among accuracy, latency, and memory. \textbf{RQ5} studies robustness as the tool catalog grows from $3$ to $1{,}000$ entries.

\subsection{Contributions}
We construct two benchmarks: the Canonical Core Benchmark with 77,028 paired English--Vietnamese records and CustomTools-VI with 8,000 samples, unseen-tool partitions, and 50\% negative queries in both test splits. We compare an end-to-end generative SLM with a schema-aware Bi-Encoder--Cross-Encoder architecture and evaluate two commercial APIs on CustomTools-VI. Finally, a stress test examines catalogs from $N=3$ to $N=1{,}000$, reporting accuracy, latency, memory, and execution failures; measurements obtained under different protocols are compared descriptively.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Tool Calling with Generative Language Models}
Toolformer examines how language models can learn to invoke tools \cite{schick2023toolformer}, whereas Gorilla focuses on API selection from documentation \cite{patil2023gorilla}. APIGen/xLAM \cite{liu2024apigen} and ToolACE \cite{liu2024toolace} scale automatically generated and verified function-calling data. The Berkeley Function-Calling Leaderboard (BFCL) provides evaluation protocols covering different call structures and dialogue settings \cite{yan2024bfcl}.

Ersoy et al. investigate SLM fine-tuning for Arabic and report benefits from combining translated and native-language data in their setting \cite{ersoy2025tool}. We investigate a corresponding question for Vietnamese while adding a Bi-Encoder--Cross-Encoder comparison and controlled bilingual data splits.

\subsection{Dense Retrieval and Discriminative Argument Extraction}
For systems with many APIs, placing every tool definition in the model context increases input length and inference cost. ToolLLM \cite{qin2024toolllm} and AnyTool \cite{du2024anytool} study retrieval or selection mechanisms that reduce the candidate set before subsequent processing. BGE-M3 provides multilingual representations and supports dense retrieval at multiple granularities \cite{chen2024bgem3}.

Prior approaches build on bidirectional BERT representations \cite{devlin2019bert} for intent and slot prediction, including JointBERT \cite{chen2019jointbert} and Schema-Guided Dialogue \cite{rastogi2020schema}. XLM-RoBERTa supplies a cross-lingual encoder backbone for argument extraction \cite{conneau2020xlmr}. Unlike pipelines that retain a generative model after retrieval, our system constructs calls with schema-routed classification and extraction heads, without autoregressive output decoding.

\subsection{Translation Rules and Frozen Revision}
To limit leakage and preserve identical partitions across models, revision \texttt{2026-09-02-full-dedup-seed42} was frozen before evaluation. It contains 77,028 paired records after deduplication, partitioned approximately 80/10/10 with a fixed seed. Function names, parameter keys, UUIDs, currency codes, and schema structure remain unchanged in translation; only queries, tool descriptions, and natural-language string values are translated.

\subsection{CustomTools-VI}
CustomTools-VI comprises 8,000 instances across ten service domains: e-commerce, domestic transport and travel, food, utilities, digital banking, real estate, public administration, healthcare, education, and postal services. Tasks include order tracking, ticket booking, bill payment, bank transfers, rental searches, traffic-violation lookup, and medical appointments.

\textbf{Data construction and quality control.} The procedure comprises four stages: manual design of 40 schemas with \texttt{string}, \texttt{integer}, \texttt{number}, \texttt{boolean}, and \texttt{enum} types; prompted generation of conversational variants; automatic checks of schema conformance and required fields; and manual review of all 1,600 test instances for gold labels, naturalness, and negative-sample balance. These steps reduce detectable errors but do not guarantee an error-free benchmark.

\textbf{Unseen-tool split.} Twenty unseen tools across ten domain groups are absent from training examples and hard-negative mining for both methods; their schemas are provided only at inference time. Each of \texttt{test\_seen} and \texttt{test\_unseen} contains 800 samples, evenly split between tool-calling and non-tool queries.

\subsection{Reproducibility}
Each experiment is linked to a configuration, frozen data revision, and seed. Source code, manifests, data checksums, training configurations, and checkpoints are intended for release under appropriate licenses. Resource links will be added according to the selected venue's review policy.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Method 1: End-to-End SLM}
We formulate tool calling as conditional sequence generation. The input consists of a system prompt specifying custom tags recognized by a study-specific parser, the available tool catalog $\mathcal{T}=\{t_1,t_2,\dots,t_K\}$ with detailed JSON schemas, and the user's natural-language query $q$.

\subsubsection{Response Format}
The model is trained to generate custom tags rather than standard XML:
\begin{verbatim}
<tool_call>
<function=tool_name>
<parameter=param_name>
param_value
</parameter>
</function>
</tool_call>
\end{verbatim}
When no tool is required, it generates a natural conversational response (e.g., \textit{``Hello! How may I help you today?''}) rather than a special token such as \texttt{<no\_tool\_call>}.

\subsubsection{Response-Only Loss}
To focus learning on the output structure rather than reproducing the tool definitions in the prompt, loss is computed only on assistant response tokens:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{SFT}=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_i\log P(w_i\mid w_{<i},q,\mathcal{T}).
\]
Here $m_i=1$ if token $w_i$ belongs to the assistant response and $m_i=0$ for system, tool-schema, and user-query tokens.

\subsubsection{Experimental Configurations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{E0 (zero-shot baseline):} The original \texttt{unsloth/Qwen3.5-2B} checkpoint, without project-specific SFT.
    \item \textbf{E1 (English only):} 60,000 English Core samples.
    \item \textbf{E2 (Vietnamese only):} The corresponding 60,000 Vietnamese Core samples.
    \item \textbf{E3 (balanced bilingual):} 30,000 English and 30,000 Vietnamese samples.
    \item \textbf{E4 (bilingual and domain-specific):} E3's 60,000 bilingual samples plus 5,600 CustomTools-VI training instances, for 65,600 in total.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Method 2: Bi-Encoder--Cross-Encoder}
This two-stage system constructs structured outputs without autoregressive decoding.

\subsubsection{Stage 1: Bi-Encoder Tool Retrieval}
The Bi-Encoder embeds user query $q$ and tool description $d_t$ as dense vectors $\mathbf{e}_q,\mathbf{e}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$:
\[
\mathbf{e}_q=\text{BiEncoder}(q),\qquad\mathbf{e}_t=\text{BiEncoder}(d_t).
\]
Semantic similarity is $s(q,t)=\cos(\mathbf{e}_q,\mathbf{e}_t)$. BGE-M3 is trained in two rounds with \texttt{CachedMultipleNegativesRankingLoss}. The first round uses 70,988 positive pairs; its checkpoint then retrieves highly similar incorrect tools as hard negatives for the second round.

\textbf{Abstention.} We calibrate a minimum confidence threshold $\tau=0.35$ and top-1/top-2 score margin $\delta=0.21$ on validation data, with at most $k_{\max}=3$ tools. If the scores fail the decision criteria, the system abstains from calling a tool. These thresholds are frozen before test evaluation.

\subsubsection{Stage 2: Cross-Encoder Argument Extraction}
For each accepted tool $t$, the XLM-RoBERTa Cross-Encoder processes a BERT-QA-style pair of query $q$ and parameter definition $p_k\in\mathcal{P}_t$:
\[
\mathbf{h}=\text{CrossEncoder}([CLS]\circ q\circ[SEP]\circ p_k\circ[SEP]).
\]
The model has four prediction heads:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{\texttt{has\_value}:} Binary classification of whether $p_k$ is present in the query:
    \[\hat{y}_{has}=\sigma(\mathbf{W}_{has}\mathbf{h}_{[CLS]}+b_{has}).\]
    \item \textbf{\texttt{span\_extraction}:} For free-form strings, predicts the start and end positions in the query:
    \begin{align*}P_{start}(i)&=\text{softmax}(\mathbf{W}_s\mathbf{h}_i),\\P_{end}(j)&=\text{softmax}(\mathbf{W}_e\mathbf{h}_j).\end{align*}
    \item \textbf{\texttt{enum\_classification}:} For enumerated parameters, scores permitted schema values:
    \[\mathbf{p}_{enum}=\text{softmax}(\mathbf{W}_{enum}\mathbf{h}_{[CLS]}+\mathbf{b}_{enum}).\]
    \item \textbf{\texttt{boolean}:} For Boolean parameters, predicts true or false:
    \[\hat{y}_{bool}=\sigma(\mathbf{W}_{bool}\mathbf{h}_{[CLS]}+b_{bool}).\]
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Hierarchical Multi-Task Loss}
The Cross-Encoder is jointly optimized with a conditional loss:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{Cross}=\mathcal{L}_{has}+y_{has}\sum_{b\in\{span,enum,bool\}}\mathbf{1}[b=b(p_k)]\lambda_b\mathcal{L}_b.
\]
Here $\mathcal{L}_{has}$ is binary cross-entropy for parameter presence, with $y_{has}\in\{0,1\}$. The presence label gates sub-head losses so that a branch contributes only for a present parameter of its type $b(p_k)$. The span loss sums start and end cross-entropies; enum uses multiclass cross-entropy over schema-permitted values; and Boolean uses binary cross-entropy. The branch weights are $\lambda_{span}=\lambda_{enum}=\lambda_{bool}=1.0$.

\subsubsection{Schema-Driven Inference Routing}
At inference, the system selects the head matching the JSON Schema \texttt{type} or \texttt{enum} field if $\hat{y}_{has}\geq 0.5$. Otherwise, the parameter is treated as absent, and no sub-head value is emitted. This prevents predictions of incompatible types from being combined in the output.

\subsubsection{Value Normalization}
A dictionary- and regular-expression-based post-processor converts selected Vietnamese surface forms to canonical values, for example, \texttt{20/10/2026} to \texttt{2026-10-20} when the year is explicit, or ``nửa triệu'' to \texttt{500000}.

\subsection{Hardware and Training Hyperparameters}
SLMs are trained with Unsloth and 4-bit NF4 QLoRA \cite{dettmers2023qlora} on Google Colab Pro using one NVIDIA A100-SXM4-40GB, 83.5 GB RAM, Ubuntu 22.04 LTS, CUDA 12.4, and PyTorch 2.5. The Bi-Encoder and Cross-Encoder are trained separately on a 16 GB NVIDIA Tesla T4. Inference evaluation uses a Kaggle environment with two T4 GPUs, although each SLM query runs on only one GPU. SLM inference uses 4-bit NF4 quantization, greedy decoding, and \texttt{max\_new\_tokens=128}; Method 2 runs on one T4 at batch size 1.

In separate T4 measurements, the 4-bit Qwen3.5-2B has reference peak VRAM of 4.8--5.5 GB, depending on allocator overhead; Qwen3.5-4B uses approximately 9.5 GB. Method 2 stress-test telemetry reports 3,277.14--3,283.09 MiB peak PyTorch allocated memory (approximately 3.20--3.21 GiB) and 3,388--3,772 MiB peak reserved memory. The SLM and Method 2 values reflect different profilers and allocation scopes and are compared descriptively.

SLM QLoRA uses $r=16$, $\alpha=16$, zero dropout, learning rate $5\times10^{-7}$ (cosine schedule, 0.05 warmup), effective batch size 64, and q, k, v, o, gate, up, and down Linear modules. BGE-M3 uses LoRA ($r=16$, $\alpha=32$, dropout 0.05), learning rate $2\times10^{-5}$, effective batch size 256, and two CachedMNRL rounds with hard negatives. XLM-R uses hierarchical heads and effective batch size 64, with learning rates $3\times10^{-5}$ on Core, $1\times10^{-5}$ on Custom, and $1\times10^{-4}$ for prediction heads. Training time and final loss are discussed in Section~6.7.

\section{Metrics and Evaluation Setup}
Following structured-call evaluation in BFCL \cite{yan2024bfcl} and Ersoy et al. \cite{ersoy2025tool}, we use the following metrics across test splits.

\subsection{Tool Selection Accuracy}
\textbf{Tool Selection Accuracy (Tool Acc, \%)} is the fraction of positive queries whose predicted tool-name list exactly matches the reference, regardless of argument values. The local-model scorer requires both membership and order for multi-call queries. The stored API evaluator sorts predicted and reference tool names before comparing, retaining multiplicity but not order. API Tool Acc is thus a protocol-specific comparison rather than an order-equivalent score:
\[\mathrm{ToolAcc}=\frac{N_{\mathrm{tool\text{-}exact,positive}}}{N_{\mathrm{positive}}}.\]

\subsection{Exact-Match Argument Accuracy}
\textbf{ArgA} measures the proportion of queries whose entire predicted call list exactly matches the reference, including function names, parameter keys, and extracted values. The local scorer matches calls in order and applies study-specific normalization. The API scorer matches each predicted call to an unmatched reference regardless of order; it uses a numeric tolerance of $10^{-4}$ and trims whitespace and ignores case for other values. API and local ArgA are thus descriptive comparisons, particularly for multi-call queries.

The default metric across all test queries, including correctly abstained negative examples, is
\[\mathrm{ArgA}_{\mathrm{all}}=\frac{N_{\mathrm{exact,all}}}{N_{\mathrm{all}}}.\]
For positive queries requiring tool calls, we also report
\[\mathrm{ArgA}_{\mathrm{positive}}=\frac{N_{\mathrm{exact,positive}}}{N_{\mathrm{positive}}}.\]
Unless explicitly stated otherwise, ``ArgA'' denotes $\mathrm{ArgA}_{\mathrm{all}}$ throughout.

\subsection{Non-FC Recall}
This metric measures abstention on ordinary conversational queries that require no tool call:
\[\mathrm{Non\text{-}FC\ Recall}=\frac{N_{\mathrm{true\ negative}}}{N_{\mathrm{negative}}}.\]

\subsection{Syntax Errors and Latency}
The syntax-error rate counts tool-call outputs not recognized by the parser associated with each method. SLMs use custom tags and a dedicated parser; APIs may use JSON. The stored API evaluator also flags failures in its exception branch, so its rate is not identical to the SLM format check. This metric does not test XML validity or argument correctness. Method 2 assembles structured outputs directly and records 0.00\% format errors under the current protocol; content correctness is evaluated by ArgA. Tables distinguish means from P50/P95 values. On Core, SLM latency is batch generation time divided by the sample count, whereas Method 2 measures single queries at batch size 1. The stress test likewise reports Method 2 P50/P95 and SLM batch-normalized time. Measurements from different protocols are compared descriptively, without computing direct speedup factors.

\subsection{RQ1: Cross-Lingual Transfer}
Comparing E1 (English only) with E2 (Vietnamese only), E1 remains effective on Vietnamese schema-guided queries. On Core VI, E1 reaches 65.57\% ArgA-all against 64.90\% for E2; on Custom Unseen, the scores are 70.50\% and 59.62\%, respectively. These results are consistent with partial transfer of schema interpretation, although the experiment does not isolate multilingual pretraining from fine-tuning effects.

\subsection{RQ2: Over-Triggering and Negative Examples}
Bilingual E3 degrades markedly on CustomTools-VI: ArgA-all is 19.38\% on seen and 27.88\% on unseen, while Non-FC Recall is only 3.00\% and 2.00\%. Most negative queries are mistaken for tool requests, indicating over-triggering in this evaluation setting.

After E4 adds 5,600 CustomTools-VI samples containing negatives, Non-FC Recall reaches 100.00\% on both splits and ArgA-all increases to 87.00\% on seen and 86.38\% on unseen. This difference is consistent with in-domain data improving abstention, but E3 and E4 differ in multiple data components, so the contribution of negative examples alone is not isolated.

\subsection{RQ3: Generalization to Unseen Tools}
SLM E4 reaches 86.38\% ArgA-all on unseen tools, 0.62 percentage points below its seen result. This is consistent with using tool descriptions and schemas supplied in the prompt, although the individual mechanisms have not been isolated. Method 2 drops from 85.38\% on seen to 60.25\% on unseen; unseen ArgA-positive is 26.75\% and Tool Acc is 79.50\%. Oracle evaluation shows that errors extend beyond retrieval, without attributing the full decline exclusively to the Cross-Encoder.

\subsection{RQ4: Quality, Latency, and Memory Trade-Off}
On CustomTools-VI, SLM 2B E4 obtains 87.00\% seen and 86.38\% unseen ArgA, compared with 85.38\% and 60.25\% for Method 2. SLM 2B E4 has 970 ms batch-normalized latency on Core VI; Method 2 has 55.05--107.68 ms batch-1 P50 across stress-test settings. Reference SLM peak VRAM is approximately 4.8--5.5 GB, compared with approximately 3.21 GiB allocated and up to 3.77 GiB reserved for Method 2 in the stress test. Timing and memory measurements follow different protocols and are compared descriptively. Across CustomTools-VI, the SLM syntax-error rate is 2.44\%, versus 0.00\% for Method 2 under its structured-output definition; this metric does not replace ArgA. SLM E4 may be appropriate when new schemas are common, whereas Method 2 may be appropriate when the catalog is comparatively stable and low batch-1 latency is a priority.

\subsection{Comparison with Commercial APIs}
We compare the local models with two commercial APIs on 1,600 CustomTools-VI instances.

\textbf{GPT-5.6 Luna.} Tool Acc is 93.25\% on seen and 97.25\% on unseen, with 100.00\% Non-FC Recall. ArgA-all is 78.25\% and 79.50\%, respectively. Qwen3.5-2B E4 reaches 87.00\% and 86.38\%, higher by 8.75 and 6.88 percentage points under the respective scorers. The stored API scorer does not preserve multi-call order and allows numeric tolerance; these are therefore descriptive comparisons, not scores recomputed with one common scorer. A single benchmark and API run cannot attribute the difference solely to fine-tuning or establish generalization to other domains.

\textbf{Gemini 3.8 Flash.} Tool Acc reaches 100.00\% on both splits; ArgA-all is 93.62\% on seen and 92.12\% on unseen. Kaggle logs report 0/800 syntax errors on seen and 1/800 (0.12\%) on unseen, for a combined 1/1,600 (0.06\%).

\textbf{Latency, cost, and data governance.} Commercial APIs, SLMs, and Method 2 were measured under different protocols; their values are descriptive comparisons rather than normalized speedup or cost ratios. Commercial APIs depend on internet connectivity and provider pricing, whereas local models require separate infrastructure assessment.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}

\subsection{Error Analysis}
\subsubsection{Vietnamese Argument-Extraction Errors}
Review of incorrect Method 1 E4 and Method 2 predictions on Vietnamese queries suggests three types of argument error. Because no auditable error-labeling sample and coding procedure is available, their prevalence is not estimated. Multi-call ordering is treated separately as a structural issue.

\textbf{Entity-boundary ambiguity.} Multi-level addresses and complex named entities may be truncated. The Cross-Encoder span head can omit part of an entity, while the SLM may add connectors or change spelling relative to the query.

\textbf{Noncanonical expressions.} Monetary amounts, dates, license plates, and identifiers may require conversion to schema-specific canonical forms. The Value Normalizer covers some common patterns but can still miss values or convert them to the wrong type.

\textbf{Implicit argument assignment.} When an optional value is absent, the generative model may supply a guessed value while the gold label leaves it empty. Method 2's \texttt{has\_value} head may mitigate this error, but its contribution has not been measured separately.

\subsubsection{Bi-Encoder--Cross-Encoder Diagnostics}
We examine oracle retrieval, individual heads, and structural constraints to understand Method 2's decline on unseen tools and Core.

\textbf{Oracle evaluation.} Given the reference tool name, Method 2 ArgA-all rises from 30.26\% to 36.81\% on Core VI, 35.52\% to 42.23\% on Core EN, 85.38\% to 92.00\% on Custom Seen, and 60.25\% to 64.25\% on Custom Unseen. Positive-only unseen ArgA rises from 26.75\% to 28.50\%. The result is consistent with extraction on new schemas being an important bottleneck, but this oracle is not a perfect upper bound for repeated calls and does not assign the entire decline to one component.

\textbf{Validation-head breakdown.} The Cross-Encoder achieves \texttt{has\_value} F1 of 97.45\%, span exact match of 96.18\%, and Boolean accuracy of 98.90\%. Enum accuracy is 72.06\% and argument exact match is 65.65\%, below the respective anticipated thresholds of 90\% and 70\%. Among the reported heads, enum prediction is the weakest.

\textbf{Structural call constraints.} The Core Benchmark contains 1,171 queries whose reference calls repeat a tool name (e.g., two \texttt{add\_item} calls with different arguments) and 146 queries with more than three calls. Method 2 maps each tool name at most once and imposes $k_{\max}=3$, preventing exact representation of these cases.

\subsection{Limitations}
\textbf{Single-turn scope.} The study examines single-turn queries that may contain multiple calls; multi-turn state tracking is outside its scope.

\textbf{Tool execution.} Calls are evaluated against reference annotations for schema and argument matching, without executing actual APIs in a sandbox or evaluating their responses.

\textbf{Nonuniform resource measurement.} Reference SLM peak VRAM (approximately 4.8--5.5 GB for 2B and 9.5 GB for 4B) and Method 2 PyTorch peak VRAM in the stress test (approximately 3.21 GiB allocated, 3.77 GiB reserved) are recorded under different environments and profiler definitions. Latency and memory comparisons are descriptive rather than statistically controlled speedup or savings estimates.

\textbf{Statistical uncertainty.} Tables report point estimates from one run without confidence intervals or multiple seeds. Small differences are therefore interpreted only for the evaluated test sets.

\section{Conclusion}
We construct two benchmarks and compare an end-to-end SLM with a Bi-Encoder--Cross-Encoder architecture for Vietnamese tool calling. On CustomTools-VI, Qwen3.5-2B E4 reaches 87.00\% ArgA on seen and 86.38\% on unseen tools; Qwen3.5-4B E4 reaches 86.62\% and 86.75\%, respectively. In the stress test, Method 2 records 84.00\% ArgA, 107.68 ms P50, and approximately 3.21 GiB PyTorch allocated VRAM at $N=1{,}000$, while the SLM encounters CUDA OOM from $N\geq500$ on a 16 GB T4. These results characterize quality, latency, and scalability trade-offs under the stated protocols rather than universal superiority of either architecture.

Future work should evaluate multiple seeds and confidence intervals, align profilers across methods, and extend evaluation to multi-turn conversations with actual tool execution. A hybrid system using the Bi-Encoder to narrow the schemas supplied to an SLM should also be tested under a common protocol for accuracy, latency, and memory.
